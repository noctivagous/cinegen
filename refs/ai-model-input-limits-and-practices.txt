**Input limits and constraints vary significantly** across major AI video generation platforms in 2026. These directly impact consistency workflows, as longer prompts, more references, or extended durations can strain coherence. Most tools favor concise, structured inputs over exhaustive ones for reliable results. Here’s a breakdown focused on standard (non-custom) models:

### Prompt Length Limits
- **Runway Gen-4 / Gen-4.5**: Maximum ~1000 characters for text prompts. Shorter, focused prompts perform better for motion and consistency.
- **Luma Dream Machine / Ray**: Up to 5000 characters, but very long prompts can lead to ignored details or errors. Minimum 3 characters; aim for concise descriptions.
- **Kling (3.0 and similar)**: No strict published character limit in most docs, but practical advice emphasizes short, action-focused prompts (action + environment + camera). Overly detailed prompts reduce effectiveness—use negatives and references instead for consistency.
- **Pika Labs**: Prefers short, clear prompts (subject + action + setting + style). Long, detailed prompts often get ignored or produce muddled results. No hard token limit publicized, but brevity is key.

**General tip**: Even with higher limits, models handle 200–800 characters most reliably for consistent outputs. Break complex scenes into parameters like camera movement, lighting, and subject action rather than cramming everything in.

### Reference Image / Asset Limits (Critical for Consistency)
- **Runway Gen-4 References**: Up to **3 reference images** per generation. Excellent for character/location locking. Supports various resolutions (e.g., max ~1280x720 for 16:9).
- **Kling**: Strong multi-reference support—often **3–4 images** (or up to more via Elements/Subject Binding). Can use front/side/profile + outfit details. Element references and "Bind Subject" features excel at identity retention without custom training.
- **Luma**: Supports reference/keyframe images (start/end frames common). Multi-image prompting available, but limits aren't as explicitly high as Kling/Runway—typically 1–several for personalization.
- **Pika**: Good image-to-video and keyframe tools (Pikaframes), but consistency across clips relies more on chaining than heavy multi-ref in single gens. Image uploads have size limits (e.g., ~10MB).

**Optimal practice**: Provide 1–4 high-quality, varied-angle references (well-lit, clear faces) rather than overloading. More references improve consistency but can slow generation or introduce conflicts if they clash.

### Video Duration & Generation Constraints
Most tools cap single generations at short clips, requiring chaining/extension for longer content (which tests consistency):
- **Runway**: Often 5–10 seconds (up to ~16s in some modes). Extensions possible.
- **Kling**: Up to 10–15 seconds standard; some modes reach 30s+ or longer with stitching. Strong temporal consistency in longer clips.
- **Luma**: ~5–10 seconds per generation; extensions add 2–5s each. Can chain to 30s+.
- **Pika**: 5–10 seconds typical (earlier versions 3–5s).

Other constraints: Resolution (720p–1080p common, some 4K), credits per second, aspect ratios (16:9, 9:16, 1:1, etc.), and generation queues. Negative prompts help lock consistency by excluding unwanted changes.

### Supported Camera Angles & Shots
Models understand standard film terminology reliably. Common ones include:

- **Aerial / Bird's Eye / Overhead**: High overhead views, often drone-like, showing scale or landscapes.
- **High Angle**: Camera looks down, making subjects appear smaller or vulnerable.
- **Low Angle**: Camera looks up, conveying power or grandeur.
- **Eye Level / Neutral**: Natural human perspective for relatability.
- **Ground Level / Worm's Eye**: Extreme low shots for dramatic effect.
- **Over-the-Shoulder (OTS)**: From behind a character, ideal for dialogue or point-of-view.
- **POV / Selfie / First-Person**: Immersive, direct viewer perspective.
- **Close-Up / Medium Shot / Wide Shot / Establishing Shot**: Varying field of view (framing) from intimate details to broad context.

**Framing/Field of View** works best when specified with lens-like terms (e.g., "50mm lens, medium shot," "wide-angle 24mm establishing shot"). This helps control depth and focus naturally.

### Camera Movements
These are well-supported across tools, especially in **Runway Gen-4.5**, **Kling 3.0+**, and **Luma Ray**:

- **Static / Locked / Fixed Camera**: "Still camera," "tripod shot," "zero movement" for maximum consistency.
- **Pan (Left/Right)**: Horizontal rotation from a fixed point.
- **Tilt (Up/Down)**: Vertical rotation.
- **Zoom (In/Out)**: Changing focal length smoothly.
- **Dolly / Push In / Pull Out / Tracking**: Camera physically moves through space toward/away from or following a subject. Excellent for depth and parallax.
- **Orbit / Arc / Crane**: Circular or rising movements around subjects.
- **Pedestal / Elevator**: Vertical lift or drop.
- **Truck / Slide**: Lateral movement parallel to the scene.
- **Roll**: Rotational tilt for dynamic or disorienting effects.
- **Handheld / FPV / Shaky Cam**: Organic, realistic motion.
- **Advanced**: 360° spins, bolt cam, tiny planet effects, or combined moves (e.g., "slow dolly forward while tilting up").

**Runway** excels with granular controls like Motion Brush for directing specific elements alongside camera paths. **Kling** shines in complex tracking and physics-aware motion. **Luma** has strong preset concepts for reliable reproduction.

- **Combine with References**: Use start/end frames or character refs to anchor shots. Explicitly reference angles in initial images.
- **Negatives**: Add "no camera shake, no random movement, stable framing" to lock consistency.
- **Chaining**: Generate short clips (5–10s) and extend using the last frame as input, carrying over the same camera directives.

### What to Include for Static Backdrops
Provide a dedicated **background plate**—a high-quality, static reference image of the backyard. This acts as your environmental anchor:

- **Key elements to capture**: The house exterior (windows, doors, siding), lawn/grass texture, trees/shrubs, fence, sky/lighting conditions (e.g., golden hour, overcast), and overall composition. Use a wide or establishing shot angle for flexibility.
- **Why this works**: Tools like Runway, Kling, and Luma maintain the background more reliably when it's supplied visually. This prevents drift (e.g., the house changing style mid-video) and lets people/props move naturally on top.
- **Tips for optimal results**: Generate or photograph the backdrop with even lighting, no people, and consistent weather/season. Include subtle depth (foreground bushes, mid-ground patio, background trees) for parallax in camera moves. Reuse the exact same plate across chained clips.

In prompts, reference it explicitly (e.g., "in the established backyard from the reference image") alongside character refs.

### Handling Props via Sprite Sheets or Arrays
AI tools don't handle traditional sprite sheets (grid of isolated props) particularly well in one go—they can interpret them as cluttered or separate entities, leading to blending, duplication, or ignored details.

- **Better alternatives**:
  - **Separate prop references**: Upload 1–3 individual high-quality images of key props (e.g., one for a grill, one for lawn chairs, one for toys/scattered items). Many platforms support multi-image references (Kling Elements, Runway References) and let you label or weight them.
  - **Composite prop plate**: Create a single clean image with arranged props (not a rigid sprite grid) placed in realistic backyard positions or on a transparent/neutral background. This gives spatial context.
  - **Strength/weight**: Set higher adherence for critical props to lock their appearance without overpowering motion.

**Outcome**: The AI integrates them more naturally into the scene rather than treating them as a sheet to parse. Test with 2–4 total references (1 background + 1–2 characters + 1 props) to stay within typical limits.

1. **Create a Reference Library**:
   - Background plate (backyard).
   - Character sheets (multiple angles, outfits matching the scene).
   - Prop references.
   - Style/lighting reference (e.g., warm afternoon light, realistic photography style).

2. **Image-to-Video Foundation**:
   - Start with a high-quality static composite image: People posed naturally in the backyard with props placed. Use this as the primary input for image-to-video modes. This locks composition better than text alone.

3. **Prompt Structure**:
   - "Static wide shot of the established backyard [describe key features briefly], characters [exact descriptors] interacting naturally with props, consistent lighting from reference, subtle leaf movement, no changes to environment."
   - Add camera: "Static camera" or gentle movements like "slow pan right" for backyard feel.

4. **Chaining for Longer Scenes**:
   - Generate short clips (5–10s).
   - Use the last frame of one as the start image for the next, carrying all references forward.
   - This preserves the static backdrop while allowing action.

5. **Advanced Controls**:
   - Negative prompts: "blurry background, changing house architecture, floating props, inconsistent lighting."
   - Depth/layering: Describe foreground/mid/background to help with occlusion (people behind chairs, etc.).

**Multi-view character sheet composition** is one of the most practical ways to maximize consistency within the typical 3–4 reference image limits of today’s AI video tools. It condenses essential visual information about a character into a single, efficient anchor image that models like **Runway Gen-4**, **Kling 3.0+ (Elements)**, **Luma**, and others can draw from effectively.

### Why Multi-View Sheets Work Well
A well-composed sheet gives the model comprehensive “visual DNA” — 
facial structure from multiple angles, body proportions, clothing details, and lighting consistency — without burning multiple reference slots. This supports natural motion, turns, camera orbits, and expression changes while reducing drift.

### Optimal Layouts and Composition Rules
**Standard recommended structure** (fits cleanly in one high-resolution image):

- **Two-row layout** (most popular and effective):
  - **Top row**: 4 full-body standing views side-by-side — Front, Left profile (facing left), Right profile (facing right), Back view.
  - **Bottom row**: 3–4 close-up portraits aligned under the bodies — Front face, Left profile face, Right profile face (sometimes add a 3/4 or neutral expression variant).

- **Alternative column-based layout**:
  - Vertical columns, each containing a full-body view on top and matching close-up portrait directly below (e.g., 3–4 columns for front, 3/4, side, back).

**Key preparation principles**:
- **Clean, neutral background**: Plain white, light gray, or transparent. Avoid any environmental elements that could bind accidentally to the character.
- **Consistent lighting and style**: Even studio-style lighting across all views. Match exact outfit, hair, accessories, and rendering quality.
- **Clear spacing and labeling** (optional but helpful): Add subtle text labels like “Front View,” “Left Profile” below each section for prompt reinforcement.
- **Resolution and aspect**: Generate at high resolution (e.g., 2048px+ width) in 16:9 or square to preserve detail when uploaded.
- **Pose neutrality**: Relaxed standing poses for bodies; neutral to slight smile for faces. Avoid extreme action poses in the base sheet.

### Creation Workflow
1. **Start with a strong seed image** — Generate or upload one high-quality frontal or 3/4 view of the character.
2. **Use an image model or tool** to expand it into the sheet (e.g., via prompts in Midjourney, Flux, Leonardo, Nano Banana, or similar). Example prompt structure:
   - “Create a professional character reference sheet based strictly on the uploaded reference image. Clean neutral background, studio lighting. Top row: full-body front, left profile, right profile, back views. Bottom row: matching close-up portraits. Exact same character, outfit, and style.”
3. **Refine if needed** — Upscale, clean edges, or composite manually in a simple editor for perfect alignment.

### Usage in Video Generation
- Upload the sheet as **one reference image**.
- In prompts, reference it clearly: “the character from the reference sheet, front view in this scene...”

**Chaining** is the essential technique for creating longer, coherent videos in current AI generation tools. Most platforms (Runway Gen-4/4.5, Kling 3.0+, Luma Dream Machine, etc.) produce short clips (typically 5–15 seconds), so you build extended sequences by intelligently linking them while preserving character identity, environment, lighting, and motion flow.

### Core Chaining Methods
- **Last Frame Continuation (Most Common & Effective)**:  
  Export the final frame of one clip and upload it as the **start image** for the next generation. This provides the strongest visual anchor for temporal continuity. Combine it with your full scene kit (character sheet, background plate, props) to minimize drift.

- **Image-to-Video (I2V) Chaining**:  
  Generate or composite a high-quality static keyframe (using your multi-view character sheet + background plate), then animate it. Feed the output's end frame into the next I2V generation. This is more reliable than pure text-to-video for backyard house scenes crossing interior/exterior.

- **Extend / Continue Features**:  
  Some tools have built-in "extend" buttons that automatically continue from the previous clip. Runway and Kling often perform well here, while manual last-frame methods give more control in others.

- **Reference Persistence**:  
  Reuse the exact same character sheet (multi-view composite) and background plates across every clip. Prompt with consistent language (e.g., "the character from the reference sheet continues the action seamlessly"). Kling’s Elements binding and Runway References shine for this.

### Best Practices for Strong Consistency
- **Short, Focused Clips**: Aim for 5–10 seconds per segment with one primary action or camera move. This reduces internal drift and makes transitions smoother.
- **Prompt Discipline**: Keep core descriptors locked ("same warm golden hour lighting from the exterior plate," "character wearing the same casual outfit"). Focus new prompts on what *changes* (action, subtle camera pan) rather than re-describing everything.
- **Negative Prompts**: Use them to fight common issues — "no facial changes, no architecture drift, no sudden lighting shifts, seamless continuation."
- **Transitions**: For interior/exterior moves, create a dedicated threshold reference (character at doorway) as a bridging frame. Gentle camera moves (slow pan, subtle dolly) chain better than abrupt ones.
- **Post-Processing**: Stitch in tools like CapCut or Premiere for final polish, color grading, and audio.

### Scene Kit Integration for Chaining

- Load the primary exterior/interior background plates as needed.
- Always include the multi-view character sheet in the reference slots.
- Save "continuation templates" in the app that auto-apply the previous end frame + full reference set.

**In your app**: Build smart chaining workflows — one-click "Continue from Last" that carries references, seeds (when available), and prompt templates forward. Preview potential drift before generating. Offer auto-compositing of the last frame into the next start image.

Chaining rewards patience and structure: each segment builds upon the solid foundation of the last, creating a natural progression where the steady environment and anchored character allow action to unfold with greater freedom and coherence, much like a flowing current guided by firm banks.
